Knowledge of HIV and behavior among women in Uganda: Analysis of DHS data, 2016

Introduction

The human immuno-deficiency virus (HIV) is the causative pathogen of the highly infectious disease associated with the Acquired Immuno-deficiency Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS). Since its emergence in the mid-1990’s, AIDS has been a leading cause of death in Uganda. Women and children are disproportionately affected by the disease, which also has huge social and economic implications. Various interventions have been put in place by both government and private actors to prevent the spread of the virus, and to curb the progression to AIDS among those already infected with the virus. Such interventions include: voluntary testing, use of condoms, mass sensitization and treatment of those infected with the virus, among others. The Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) conducted in the country in 2016 interviewed respondents across the country on their knowledge of TB, and other factors facilitating transmission and stigma. We analyzed the DHS dataset with the objectives of determining the distribution of respondents who had never heard about TB, distribution of knowledge and stigma among various respondent characteristics.

Methods

Study site

The study was conducted in Uganda, which is divided into fifteen regions. The country has a population of 45.9 million persons as per the 2024 Uganda National Population and Housing Census, 2024.

We used the following packages in R Studio to assist the various aspects of data analysis: tidyverse and dplyr for general and specific data wrangling functions respectively, haven and here packages to load Stata data into R, ggplot for visualization, and janitor for data cleaning.

Variables selected

From the DHS Individual Recode (IR) dataset, we selected the following variables. #Basic demographic data included: 1. Age of the respondent 2. Highest level of education 3. Wealth index 4. Region of residence, and 5. Whether the place of residence was urban or rural. We also selected HIV-specific data, including whether the respondents: 1. Had heard about AIDS, 2. Had basic HIV knowledge on HIV; and for these were selected the following variables: a. If the respondent knows that condom use can reduce HIV risk, b. If the respondent knows that having one uninfected sexual partner reduce the risk of contracting HIV c. If the respondent believes mosquitoes can transmit HIV d. If the respondent believes that sharing food transmits HIV e. If the respondent knows a healthy-looking person can have HIV f. If the respondent knows that HIV can be transmitted during pregnancy, g. If the respondent knows that HIV can be transmitted during delivery, and h. If the respondent knows that drugs can reduce mother-to-child HIV transmission. Other variables were behavioral including: 1. Condom use during last intercourse, and 2. If the respondent had ever tested for HIV.

Data analysis

We generated a variable called “Knowledge on HIV” and categorized it based on the number of positive responses of the nine (09) knowledge question given by a respondent. If the respondent had >6 positive responses, they were considered “Highly knowledgeable”, 5-6 positive responses were considered “Knowledgeable”, those who had 3-4 positive responses were considered “Somehow knowledgeable” and those who had 2 or less positive responses were “Not knowledgeable”. We then analyzed the data based on the following: 1. Each of the demographic variables against “Ever heard about HIV” 2. Knowledge on HIV against selected demographic variables 3. Behavior against selected demographic variables.

Relationship between age and ever hearing about AIDS, knowledge about HIV, testing and condom use behavior disaggregated by place of residence

Age and ever hearing about AIDS

Awareness of AIDS generally increased with age in both urban and rural settings. While there was a similar range among the ages among those who responded “No”, among those who responded “Yes”, there seemed a wider age variation in the urban areas.

Testing for HIV, age and educational level

Respondents with higher education and secondary education are more likely to have been tested for HIV. Their median ages are in the mid‑20s, and the age range is relatively narrow. Respondents with primary education show moderate testing rates, with median ages around the mid‑20s to early 30s. No education group stands out among both tested and untested respondents are older on average, with medians in the mid‑30s and a wide spread of ages. HIV testing increases with both age and educational attainment. Individuals with secondary or higher education are more likely to have undergone testing, possibly reflecting better awareness, access to health services, and exposure to HIV prevention campaigns. (Figure 3).

### Condom use by age and wealth index Respondents from higher wealth quintiles (fourth and highest) tend to be older and show slightly higher condom use compared to those in lower quintiles. Those who reported using condoms (“yes”) are generally younger, with median ages in the early to mid‑20s, while non‑users (“no”) have higher median ages, around late 20s to early 30s. The spread of ages is broader among wealthier groups, suggesting more diverse sexual behavior patterns across age ranges. Condom use appears more common among younger and relatively wealthier individuals, possibly reflecting greater access to sexual health information and services (Figure 4).

# Conclusion In conclusion, this analysis of the 2016 Uganda DHS data shows that HIV knowledge and preventive behaviors among women vary significantly by age, education, and wealth. Awareness and testing rates increase with both age and educational attainment, highlighting the role of education in promoting health-seeking behavior. Condom use is more common among younger and wealthier women, suggesting socioeconomic influence on access to information and services. Rural women remain less likely to test or use condoms compared to their urban counterparts. Strengthening targeted education and outreach across all socioeconomic groups is essential to sustain HIV prevention gains.
